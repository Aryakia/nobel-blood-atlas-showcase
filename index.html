Where the idea came from

It started with a blood donation

I was donating blood when I realized how little I actually knew about blood groups. That small question eventually led me to this project.

While donating blood, I started thinking about how blood types are distributed across different populations. I knew my own blood type, but not much beyond that. Out of curiosity, I wondered whether the distribution among Nobel laureates might look different from what we normally see in the population.

My first idea was very simple: find as many Nobel laureates' blood types as possible and compare them with the world average. But the more I looked at the problem, the less comfortable I became with that comparison. Nobel laureates are not a random sample of the world's population. They are concentrated in particular countries, regions and historical periods, and blood-type frequencies also vary across populations.

That means a difference from the global average could appear even if blood type has no relationship at all with Nobel achievement. The comparison itself could create the pattern.

So the question I am interested in now is different: what blood-type distribution should we expect for the Nobel population once its composition is taken into account, and how much can we trust the small set of blood types that are publicly documented?

A fairer comparison

The benchmark should reflect who the Nobel laureates are.

If blood type and Nobel achievement are unrelated, the expected distribution should still reflect the population backgrounds represented among laureates. In principle, each laureate contributes the blood-type probabilities from the most defensible reference population available, and those probabilities are added together.

Composition-adjusted expectation Et = Σi pi,t For blood type t, add the reference probability assigned to each laureate.
Preferred reference order person-specific population evidence → appropriate regional/population reference → country proxy when justified → global fallback

This does not mean guessing ancestry from a surname, nationality, appearance or school location. If the background needed for weighting is not supported by evidence, it should remain uncertain rather than be invented.

Future analysis

There is another problem

The blood types I can find online are also a selected sample.

Some countries and cultures discuss blood type more often than others, older laureates may have very little searchable material, and famous public figures may leave a much larger documentary trail. So even after fixing the population benchmark, the people whose blood types are publicly available may still be unusual.

What I learned from building it

The interesting result so far is methodological, not biological.

I began with a question about blood type. The project ended up teaching me much more about comparison groups, weighting, missing data and source quality. The current sample is too small and too selectively documented to support a claim about Nobel achievement. What it can already show, however, is how easily a simple comparison can become misleading when the comparison population and the observed sample are both selected.

Question for a later stage Not testable yet
If I can build a much stronger dataset, does the observed ABO distribution among Nobel laureates differ from the distribution expected after accounting for population composition and selective documentation?

For now, I do not think the evidence is good enough to answer that question responsibly. The site keeps the descriptive results visible, but it also keeps that limitation visible.

Only then

Run an inferential test

The statistical comparison stays on hold until the evidence base and the comparison group are strong enough to justify it.

Current research

What the project needs now

At this stage, the main work is not hypothesis testing. It is improving the evidence base: documenting what has actually been searched, tracing claims back to their sources, resolving contradictions, and understanding who is more likely to have a blood type recorded publicly.

Methods question
How can a sparse public dataset be studied without treating missing records, repeated sources and selective documentation as if they were random?

The Nobel blood-type question is the case study. The broader value of the project is the method used to decide what can—and cannot—be concluded from incomplete public evidence.

Methodology

The missing data are part of the result

The project treats provenance and uncertainty as data structures. It does not allow a polished visualization to erase the weakness of the evidence underneath it.

01

Verify identity

Crosswalk name, year and prize field against official Nobel records.

02

Search locally

Use relevant languages and name variants; record what was searched.

03

Trace provenance

Copied celebrity lists do not become independent confirmation through repetition.

04

Grade evidence

Separate confirmed, explicit secondary reporting, unverified leads, conflicts and unknowns.

05

Publish missingness

Unknown and conflicting records stay visible beside every descriptive result.

Publication boundary

Open research does not mean publishing private submissions.

This public release intentionally excludes contact information, pending user submissions, credentials, live database contents and deployment secrets. Those are operational/private data, not part of the research corpus. The analytical dataset contains only deliberately published public-source claims and their provenance.
